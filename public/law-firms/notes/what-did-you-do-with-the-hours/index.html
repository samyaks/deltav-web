Note · 16 November 2026

How does legal billing
change when AI does
the work?

UBS now requires its outside lawyers to disclose AI use on the invoice. Most firms can show who logged in. Few can say what happened to the fee.

UBS requires outside counsel to get consent before using AI on its matters, and to state that use on the invoice. In KPMG’s 2026 survey of general counsel, 82 percent agreed that they expect outside firms to document and explain how AI is used in the work. The question has moved from a conference panel onto the bill.

What a firm can usually produce in reply is a list of logins. Two years of pilots, seat assignments and training made that list easy. It tells a client who was given access. It does not say which tool touched a matter, or what happened to the fee.

Each of those records is real, and each fails the question a client has started to ask. Usage sits with the vendor. The matter, the time and the fee sit in billing. A partner’s recollection is what remains when nobody has joined them. In a fee dispute, or on an invoice that is supposed to disclose the tool, a system record is the one that holds up.

The economic consequence is easier to see on a job the firm prices from memory. For the illustration on this site we use a made-up commercial lease review: 14.2 hours before, 8.6 on the files where a lawyer actually used the tool. These are not a client’s numbers. Five and a half hours used to be time the firm could sell.

A firm that has been paying attention has already chosen, whether or not anyone wrote the choice down. It cut the fee, and can say what margin remains. It held the fee, and the gain is sitting in the matter for anyone who lays the hours next to the bill. Or the invoice is still drawn up as though the review took fourteen hours, and the difference is written off later, or never recorded, because the draft was finished before anyone started a timer.

BigHand’s 2026 finance report found the same split across firms. Thirty-two percent cite higher profitability per matter from AI. Twenty-nine percent report fewer billable hours. Both figures can be true at once. On a fixed fee the saved time is margin. On the clock it is revenue the firm no longer has, unless someone rewrites the price.

Ask the partner who sets fees and the comparison is often missing from both reports. Innovation can show adoption, finance can show realization, and the matter is in neither. The people who bought the tools and the people who defend the fee are frequently not in the same meeting. A shorter first draft changes both of their jobs, and it is now something a client can ask to see on the invoice.

Some of the value will never appear as fewer hours on an old task. A lawyer takes work the firm used to send out, or a client commissions a review they would once have declined. That belongs on its own line. The work the firm already sells every week is where a shorter draft meets a fee someone has to stand behind. A comparison in which the hours barely move is worth having too: the tool is not in that workflow, and the firm should not promise a client that this particular job has become quicker.

What is worth putting on one page is modest. One matter type the firm already knows by heart. The hours before the tool, the hours on the files where it was used, and a sentence on whether the fee moved. No client names, documents or prompts.
