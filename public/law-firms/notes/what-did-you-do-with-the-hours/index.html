What happens to the bill when AI does the work?

Clients are starting to ask how AI was used on their matters. Most firms can show who got a seat. Few can show what that did to the hours or the fee.

In KPMG’s 2026 survey, 82 percent of general counsel said they expect outside firms to document and explain how AI is used in the work. That used to be a conference-panel question. Now it shows up on the bill.

What most firms have ready is a list of logins. After two years of pilots, seat buys, and training, that list is easy. It tells a client who was given access. It does not say which tool touched which matter, or whether the fee changed.

None of that is fake. It just does not answer the question. Usage lives in the vendor’s system. The matter, the time, and the fee live in billing. When those two never meet, the only backup is a partner’s memory. If a client pushes, or the invoice is supposed to name the tool, memory is a weak place to stand.

The money side is clearer on work the firm prices from habit. On this site we use a made-up commercial lease review: 14.2 hours before AI, 8.6 on the files where a lawyer actually used the tool. These are not a client’s numbers. They are here to make the gap concrete. Five and a half hours used to be time the firm could sell.

Once that gap shows up, the firm has already chosen something, even if nobody wrote it down. Cut the fee, and know what margin is left. Hold the fee, and the gain sits in the matter for anyone who puts the hours next to the bill. Or keep drafting the invoice as if the review still took fourteen hours, and write the difference off later, or never record it, because the draft was done before anyone started a timer.

BigHand’s 2026 finance report shows the same fork. Thirty-two percent of firms say AI raised profitability per matter. Twenty-nine percent say billable hours fell. Both can be true. On a fixed fee, saved time is margin. On the clock, it is revenue the firm no longer has, unless someone changes the price.

Ask the partner who sets fees, and that comparison is often missing from both reports. Innovation can show adoption. Finance can show realization. The matter sits in neither. The people who bought the tools and the people who defend the fee are often not in the same room. A shorter first draft changes both jobs, and a client can now ask to see it on the invoice.

Not every gain shows up as fewer hours on an old task. A lawyer may take work the firm used to send out. A client may ask for a review they would once have skipped. Count that separately. The weekly work the firm already sells is where a shorter draft meets a fee someone has to stand behind. And if the hours barely move on a matter type, that is useful too: the tool is not in that workflow, so do not promise the client this job got faster.

What to put on one page is small. One matter type the firm already knows. Hours before the tool. Hours on the files where it was used. One sentence on whether the fee moved.
